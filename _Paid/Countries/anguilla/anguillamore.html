A Brief History of Anguilla

Indigenous Peoples and Colonization (Pre-1650s)

Before European arrival, Anguilla was inhabited by Arawak and Carib peoples who lived off fishing and farming. In 1650, English settlers established plantations on the island, introducing African slaves to work the sugarcane fields. Over the next century, Anguilla changed hands between the British and French, with the Treaty of Paris in 1763 finally ceding the island to Great Britain.

Slavery, Emancipation, and Plantation Economy (1650s–1834)

The plantation economy of Anguilla relied heavily on slave labor, leading to a majority Black population. The abolition of slavery in the British Empire in 1834 brought about significant social changes, with many former slaves staying on the plantations as sharecroppers or seeking work in the salt industry, which became a major economic activity on the island.

Political Turmoil and Annexation (1834–1967)

Throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries, Anguilla faced political instability, including periods of governance from nearby islands like St. Kitts. In 1967, Anguilla seceded from the associated state of St. Kitts-Nevis-Anguilla, declaring itself an independent republic. However, this self-declared independence was short-lived as British forces intervened and Anguilla was re-annexed to the British Crown in 1971.

Modern Development and Self-Governance (1967–Present)

Since gaining a measure of self-governance in 1980, Anguilla has made significant strides in developing its tourism industry, becoming known for its pristine beaches and luxury resorts. The island has maintained a stable political environment as a British Overseas Territory, with a parliamentary system of government and a focus on sustainable development and environmental conservation.

Languages of Anguilla

Languages Spoken in Anguilla

Anguilla's official language is English, reflecting its status as a British overseas territory. English is widely used for government affairs, education, and business transactions. However, you may also hear other languages spoken on the island due to its diverse population and tourism influences.

Local Dialects and Influence

While English is the primary language, you may encounter a unique local dialect known as “Anguillian English.” This dialect may include elements of Caribbean English and Anguillian Creole, reflecting the island's cultural heritage and influences from neighboring Caribbean nations.

Respecting Language and Culture

When visiting Anguilla, it is appreciated when travelers make an effort to learn a few basic phrases in English or the local dialect. This shows respect for the local culture and enhances your overall experience on the island. Locals are usually friendly and willing to assist with language barriers.

Anguilla Cuisine

Cuisine

Anguilla may be a small island, but its culinary scene is mighty. Influenced by African, Caribbean, and British flavors, Anguillan cuisine offers a delightful fusion of tastes and ingredients. While the dishes may seem simple at first glance, the magic lies in the freshness of the ingredients and the skillful preparation that brings out the natural flavors. Seafood takes center stage in Anguillan cooking, with dishes that celebrate the bounties of the surrounding waters.

  • Grilled Lobster – Freshly caught lobster grilled to perfection, a must-try for seafood lovers.
  • Cracked Conch – Tender conch meat marinated and fried, a popular appetizer in Anguilla.
  • Johnnycakes – Fried cornmeal bread, a staple side dish in Anguillan cuisine.
  • Rice and Peas – A flavorful dish of rice cooked with pigeon peas and coconut milk.
  • Barbecue Ribs – Succulent ribs marinated in local spices and slow-cooked to perfection.
  • Mahi Mahi – Freshly caught mahi-mahi prepared in various styles, showcasing the island's seafood prowess.
  • Ackee and Saltfish – A classic Caribbean dish of salted codfish with ackee fruit, often enjoyed at breakfast.
  • Mango Tango – A refreshing cocktail blending local mangoes with rum, a favorite among visitors.
  • Banana Bread Pudding – A delectable dessert combining ripe bananas and sweet bread, a taste of Anguillan comfort food.

Local Dining Experience

Dining in Anguilla is a laid-back affair, with many restaurants offering beachfront settings for a truly tropical experience. The island's charming beach bars, known as" shacks," serve up delicious grilled seafood and refreshing cocktails, creating a relaxed and friendly atmosphere that captures the essence of island life.

Anguilla Festivals

What are Anguilla’s most famous festivals?

Anguilla, a small Caribbean gem known for its pristine beaches and warm hospitality, also boasts vibrant and lively festivals that showcase the island's rich cultural heritage. While the island's festivities may be smaller in scale compared to other destinations, they are filled with genuine charm and a sense of community.

  • Summer Festival (The Valley, August) – Held annually in August, this festival celebrates Anguilla's culture with lively parades, vibrant music, traditional dances, and delicious local cuisine. It's a time when locals and visitors come together to enjoy the island's spirit of unity and joy.
  • Moonsplash Music Festival (Rendezvous Bay, March) – A must-visit for music lovers, this festival in March features a lineup of local and international artists performing against the backdrop of the stunning Rendezvous Bay beach. Enjoy a mix of reggae, soca, calypso, and other genres under the Caribbean stars.
  • Festival Del Mar (Island Harbour, April) – Celebrating Anguilla's deep connection to the sea, this festival in April showcases the island's maritime heritage with boat races, fishing competitions, seafood tastings, and beachside festivities. It's a feast for the senses and a true reflection of Anguilla's coastal culture.
  • Anguilla Lit Fest (Various locations, May) – Book enthusiasts will delight in this literary festival held in May, featuring local and international authors, book signings, panel discussions, and workshops. Dive into the world of words amidst the island's tranquil setting.

Festival Tips

When attending festivals in Anguilla, embrace the laid-back island pace and immerse yourself in the local customs. Plan your trip well in advance, as accommodation options may be limited during festival times. Don't miss the opportunity to savor traditional Anguillian dishes and interact with friendly locals. Remember to pack sunscreen, beachwear, and a camera to capture the colorful moments. Respect the island's culture and traditions, and you'll create unforgettable memories during your festival experience in Anguilla.
