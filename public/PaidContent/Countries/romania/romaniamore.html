A Brief History of Romania

Dacian Kingdom and Roman Conquest (Before 106 CE)

Before Romania's existence, the region was inhabited by the Dacians, a proud and warlike people known for their resistance against outside invaders. In 106 CE, the Roman Emperor Trajan launched a military campaign that led to the conquest of Dacia, incorporating it into the Roman Empire. This period brought Roman culture, infrastructure, and Latin influence to the region, shaping its future development.

Byzantine Influence and Migration Period (271–9th Century)

Following the Roman withdrawal in the 3rd century, the region came under Byzantine influence, contributing to the spread of Christianity and the development of a distinct Romanian identity. The Migration Period brought waves of nomadic tribes like the Goths, Huns, and Slavs, impacting the region's demographics and culture.

Medieval Principalities and Ottoman Rule (14th–19th Century)

The medieval era in Romania was marked by the formation of several principalities, including Wallachia, Moldavia, and Transylvania. These territories often found themselves at the crossroads of European power struggles. In the 15th century, the Ottoman Empire began its expansion into the region, leading to centuries of Ottoman rule that left a lasting impact on Romanian society.

National Awakening and Independence (19th Century)

The 19th century saw a resurgence of Romanian national identity, with cultural figures and political leaders advocating for independence from Ottoman and Habsburg rule. The revolutions of 1848 and the Congress of Berlin in 1878 played key roles in the eventual unification of Wallachia, Moldavia, and Transylvania into a single Romanian state in 1881.

World Wars and Communist Era (20th Century)

Romania experienced turbulent times during the 20th century, including participation in World War I on the side of the Allies. Following the war, the country went through periods of monarchy, authoritarianism, and eventually fell under communist rule after World War II. The communist era, under leaders like Nicolae Ceaușescu, brought about significant social and economic changes, albeit marked by repression and hardship for many Romanians.

Revolution and Post-Communist Transition (1989–Present)

The end of communist rule came in 1989 with the Romanian Revolution, leading to the overthrow of Ceaușescu's regime. Romania embarked on a path of political and economic transition, joining NATO in 2004 and the European Union in 2007. The country has since made strides in democratization, economic reforms, and modernization, while also grappling with challenges of corruption and social inequality.

Languages of Romania

Languages in Romania

Romania is a diverse country with a rich linguistic heritage. The official language is Romanian, a Romance language similar to Italian, French, and Spanish. However, there are several minority languages spoken in different regions, reflecting the country's multiculturalism and historical influences.

Minority Languages

In addition to Romanian, you may also hear Hungarian, spoken mainly in Transylvania, and German, particularly in areas with a strong historical German community. Romani, the language of the Roma minority, is also present in certain regions. Ukrainian, Serbian, and Bulgarian are spoken in border areas where these communities reside.

English Proficiency

English is increasingly spoken in Romania, especially in urban centers and among younger generations. Approximately 31% of Romanians report being able to hold a conversation in English, with higher proficiency levels in the tourism industry. In more rural or remote areas, English proficiency may be limited, so basic Romanian phrases can be helpful.

Romania Cuisine

Cuisine

Romanian cuisine is a delightful mix of flavors influenced by its history and diverse geography. While not as well-known globally as some European cuisines, Romanian dishes offer a hearty and satisfying experience that reflects the country's agricultural roots and culinary traditions. Expect dishes that are rich in meats, dairy, and vegetables, often seasoned with a touch of paprika or garlic. Traditional Romanian meals are meant to be enjoyed slowly, with family and friends, showcasing a blend of simplicity and depth of flavor.

  • Sarmale – Cabbage rolls filled with a savory mixture of minced pork, rice, and spices, often served with sour cream.
  • Mămăligă – A cornmeal porridge similar to polenta, commonly served as a side dish with stews or cheese.
  • Mici – Grilled minced meat rolls seasoned with garlic, paprika, and other spices, best enjoyed with mustard and fresh bread.
  • Ciorbă – A sour soup made with vegetables, meat, and a generous dollop of sour cream, offering a refreshing and tangy taste.
  • Mititei – Small grilled sausages made from a blend of beef, pork, and lamb, seasoned with herbs and garlic.
  • Papanași – Indulgent fried doughnuts topped with sour cream and fruit preserves, a popular dessert in Romania.
  • Zacuscă – A flavorful vegetable spread made with roasted eggplant, peppers, tomatoes, and spices, perfect for spreading on bread.
  • Varză à la Cluj – Cabbage rolls filled with a mix of pork, beef, and rice, stewed in a savory tomato sauce.
  • Salată de Vinete – A creamy eggplant salad mixed with onions, mayo, and sometimes tomatoes, served as a delicious appetizer or side dish.

Romania Festivals

What are Romania’s most famous festivals?

Romania’s rich cultural heritage is celebrated throughout the year with a variety of festivals that showcase the country's traditions and spirit. Here are some of the most iconic festivals you can experience:

  • Transylvania International Film Festival (Cluj-Napoca, May/June) – Known as TIFF, this festival screens a diverse selection of international and Romanian films, attracting filmmakers, actors, and cinema enthusiasts from around the world.
  • Sibiu International Theatre Festival (Sibiu, June) – This renowned festival features captivating performances by theater companies from various countries, set against the backdrop of Sibiu's charming medieval streets and squares.
  • George Enescu Festival (Bucharest, September) – A prestigious classical music festival honoring Romania’s greatest composer, George Enescu. Experience world-class orchestras, soloists, and chamber music performances in various venues across Bucharest.
  • Apulum Roman Festival (Alba Iulia, May) – Travel back in time to ancient Roman times at this historical reenactment festival, complete with gladiator battles, Roman legion demonstrations, and traditional Roman crafts and cuisine.
  • Dragobete (Nationwide, February 24) – Celebrated as the Romanian equivalent of Valentine’s Day, Dragobete is a day of love and spring renewal, with traditional customs, dances, and rituals observed across the country.

Festival Tips

When attending festivals in Romania, consider staying in local guesthouses or unique accommodations to immerse yourself in the culture. Check the festival schedules in advance and book tickets or accommodations early, especially for popular events. Embrace the local customs and traditions, and don’t hesitate to interact with locals to learn more about the festival's significance. Pack layers for changing weather, as some festivals may take place outdoors, and be open to trying traditional Romanian dishes and drinks to fully experience the country’s culinary delights.
