A Brief History of Eswatini

Early History and Swazi Origins (Pre-19th Century)

Long before modern-day Eswatini existed, the region was inhabited by various Bantu-speaking tribes. Among them were the Dlamini people, who would later form the core of the Swazi nation. By the early 19th century, under the leadership of King Sobhuza I, the Swazi had established themselves as a powerful group in the area, known for their fierce resistance against encroaching European powers.

Colonial Rule and British Protectorate (19th Century–1968)

In the late 19th century, the British Empire established a protectorate over Swaziland, as it was then known, to safeguard trade routes and secure access to resources. This colonial period saw significant changes in Swazi society and governance, as traditional structures clashed with European influences. Despite this, the Swazi people managed to retain much of their cultural heritage and autonomy.

Independence and Post-Colonial Challenges (1968–1990s)

On September 6, 1968, Swaziland gained independence from Britain, becoming a constitutional monarchy with King Sobhuza II as its leader. The country faced numerous challenges in the following decades, including political unrest, economic struggles, and demands for democratic reforms. King Mswati III, who ascended the throne in 1986, has faced criticism for his leadership style and the country's slow progress towards greater democracy.

Modern Eswatini and Democratic Demands (2000s–Present)

In more recent years, Eswatini has grappled with calls for political reform and greater accountability in governance. Pro-democracy movements have gained momentum, advocating for a shift towards a more pluralistic and participatory system. The government has faced international scrutiny for its handling of human rights issues and the limited political freedoms granted to its citizens. As Eswatini navigates its path forward, the balance between tradition and modernity remains a central theme in its evolving history.

Languages of Eswatini

What languages are spoken in Eswatini?

Eswatini is a multilingual country with a diverse linguistic landscape. The official language is Swati, a Bantu language widely spoken by the Swazi people. English is also an official language and is commonly used in government, education, and business. Additionally, Zulu, another Bantu language, is spoken by a significant portion of the population, especially in the eastern regions near the border with South Africa.

Language Diversity and Importance

Swati holds a special cultural significance in Eswatini as it reflects the country's heritage and traditions. Learning a few basic phrases in Swati can greatly enhance your experience and show respect for the local culture. English, on the other hand, is essential for communication in urban areas, formal settings, and with visitors from around the world.

Useful Phrases in Swati

  • Sawubona – Hello
  • Siyabonga – Thank you
  • Hamba kahle – Go well (Goodbye)
  • Ngiyaxolisa – Excuse me / Sorry
  • Unjani? – How are you?
  • Ngiyaphila – I am fine
  • Lala kahle – Good night
  • Ngicela – Please
  • Uphendule, ngiyacela – Could you please answer?
  • Ngidla kahle, ngiyabonga – I ate well, thank you
Eswatini Cuisine

Cuisine

Eswatini, a small landlocked country in Southern Africa, offers a unique culinary experience that reflects its rich cultural heritage. The cuisine here is a delightful blend of traditional African flavors, influences from neighboring countries, and colonial influences. While the dishes may not be widely known outside the region, they are a true representation of Eswatini's vibrant food culture.

  • Umgqusho – A hearty stew made with maize and beans, often flavored with vegetables and spices.
  • Sishwala – A thick porridge made from maize meal, a staple food in Eswatini.
  • Emasi – A sour milk drink that is a popular accompaniment to many meals.
  • Braaied Meats – Grilled meats such as beef, chicken, and goat, seasoned with local spices.
  • Boerewors – Traditional South African sausages, often enjoyed in Eswatini as well.
  • Marula Fruit – A seasonal delicacy used in jams, juices, and traditional brews.
  • Phutu – A crumbly maize meal dish often served with meat or vegetables.
  • Chakalaka – A spicy vegetable relish that adds flavor to many dishes.
  • Magwinya – Deep-fried dough balls, a popular street food snack.

Mealtimes

In Eswatini, mealtimes are an important part of the day and are often enjoyed with family and friends. Breakfast is typically light, with options like porridge and tea. Lunch is the main meal, usually eaten around midday, and may consist of a variety of dishes. Dinner is lighter and is commonly served in the early evening.

Eswatini Festivals

What are Eswatini’s most famous festivals?

Eswatini, formerly known as Swaziland, is a land of rich cultural traditions and vibrant celebrations. The country hosts several festivals that showcase its unique heritage and community spirit:

  • Umhlanga / Reed Dance (Ludzidzini Royal Residence, August) – A colorful ceremony where thousands of young Swazi women gather to cut reeds and present them to the Queen Mother. This cultural event celebrates chastity, unity, and the strength of the Swazi kingdom.
  • Incwala / Kingship Ceremony (Lobamba Royal Palace, December/January) – The most sacred of Swazi ceremonies, Incwala is a time of purification, prayer, and celebration of the king's divine role. It includes rituals, dances, and feasting, symbolizing the renewal of the nation.
  • Sibhaca Dance (Throughout the year, various locations) – The energetic and rhythmic Sibhaca dance is a traditional Swazi dance performed by men, showcasing agility, strength, and unity. It is often a highlight at cultural events and gatherings.
  • Marula Festival (Lubombo Region, February/March) – Celebrating the marula fruit harvest, this festival features traditional music, dance, and feasting. The marula fruit holds cultural significance in Eswatini, and the festival is a joyful celebration of abundance and community.
  • Reed Dance (Ludzidzini Royal Residence, January) – A smaller version of the main Umhlanga event, the January Reed Dance is a local celebration that offers a more intimate look at Swazi culture and traditions.

Festival Tips

When attending festivals in Eswatini, respect local customs and traditions. Dress modestly and be prepared to observe and participate respectfully. It's advisable to arrange accommodations well in advance, especially for larger events like Umhlanga and Incwala. Embrace the opportunity to learn about Swazi culture, interact with locals, and enjoy the festive atmosphere with an open heart and mind.
