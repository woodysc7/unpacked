A Brief History of American Samoa

Early Settlement and Polynesian Culture

American Samoa's history dates back over 3,000 years when the first Polynesian settlers arrived on the islands. These early settlers developed a rich Polynesian culture, with strong traditions of navigation, storytelling, and community living. The islands of American Samoa became important hubs for trade and cultural exchange among Polynesian peoples.

European Contact and Colonial Rule (18th-19th Century)

In the 18th century, European explorers, including French and British navigators, made contact with the islands. In the 19th century, the United States and Germany competed for control over Samoa. The Treaty of Berlin in 1899 divided the Samoan archipelago, with the eastern islands coming under American control, forming what we now know as American Samoa.

World Wars and American Administration (20th Century)

American Samoa played a strategic role during World War I and World War II, with the United States establishing a naval base on the islands. During this time, American influence grew, shaping the economy and governance of the territory. The signing of the Deeds of Cession in the early 20th century solidified American Samoa's status as an unincorporated territory of the United States.

Cultural Revival and Autonomy Movements (Late 20th Century)

Throughout the late 20th century, there was a renewed focus on preserving and promoting traditional Samoan culture and language. Autonomy movements gained traction, advocating for greater self-governance and preservation of Samoan identity. The 1960s and 1970s saw the establishment of a local legislature and the move towards a more autonomous political system.

Contemporary American Samoa (21st Century)

In the 21st century, American Samoa continues to navigate its unique status as an unincorporated territory of the United States. The islands face challenges such as environmental conservation, economic development, and maintaining cultural heritage in a rapidly changing world. Despite these challenges, American Samoa remains a vibrant and culturally rich destination, welcoming visitors to experience its unique blend of Polynesian traditions and American influences.

Languages of American Samoa

What languages are spoken in American Samoa?

American Samoa has two official languages: English and Samoan. English is widely used in government, education, and business, while Samoan is the language of everyday communication for the majority of the population. Understanding and using a few basic Samoan phrases can show respect for the local culture.

English in American Samoa

English is commonly spoken and understood throughout American Samoa, especially in urban areas and tourist destinations. Visitors should have no trouble communicating in English, as it is the primary language for official purposes and tourism-related services.

Basic Samoan Phrases

  • Talofa – Hello
  • Fa'amalie atu – Excuse me / Sorry
  • Leai, fa'amalie atu – No, sorry
  • Manuia le po – Good night
  • Fa'afetai – Thank you
  • Ua ou misi – I am lost
  • Fea le fale tele? – Where is the restroom?
  • O ai lou igoa? – What is your name?
  • Manuia le aso – Have a good day
  • Fa'amalie atu, ou te le fiafia – Sorry, I am not happy
American Samoa Cuisine

Cuisine

American Samoa offers a unique blend of Polynesian flavors with influences from Hawaiian, Samoan, and Asian cuisine. The local food scene may not be as widely known as other Pacific islands, but it offers a rich culinary experience that reflects the island's culture and heritage. While some dishes may appear simple, they are packed with bold and distinctive flavors that are sure to intrigue adventurous food enthusiasts.

  • Oka – A traditional Samoan dish made with raw fish marinated in coconut milk, lime juice, and vegetables like cucumber and tomatoes.
  • Palusami – Taro leaves filled with coconut milk, onion, and sometimes meat, then wrapped in banana leaves and baked to perfection.
  • Lu Sipi – A flavorful dish of corned beef cooked with taro leaves and coconut milk, a beloved comfort food in American Samoa.
  • Fa'apapa – Sweet and fluffy coconut bread rolls, often enjoyed as a snack or dessert.
  • Supoesi – A Samoan version of chop suey, featuring noodles stir-fried with vegetables and meat or seafood.
  • Poi – A staple side dish made from mashed taro root, similar to poi found in other Polynesian cultures.
  • Povi Masima – Corned beef brisket stewed with vegetables and coconut milk, a hearty and satisfying meal.
  • Palagi Suaia – Coconut milk soup with fish, taro, breadfruit, and vegetables, offering a taste of traditional Samoan flavors.
  • Puligi – A sweet dessert made with taro, bananas, coconut cream, and sugar, often baked or steamed.

Local Dining Customs

In American Samoa, food is not just about sustenance but also a way to celebrate community and tradition. Dining is often a communal affair, with locals enjoying meals together and sharing stories and laughter. Traditional cooking methods, such as umu (earth oven), are still prevalent, infusing dishes with a smoky and earthy flavor

American Samoa Festivals

What are American Samoa’s most famous festivals?

American Samoa may be a small territory in the Pacific, but its festivals are big on culture, tradition, and community spirit. Here are some of the most renowned celebrations that offer a glimpse into the vibrant soul of these islands:

  • Flag Day (Pago Pago, April 17th) – Celebrated on April 17th, Flag Day commemorates the day American Samoa became a U.S. territory. The festivities include traditional dance performances, music, sports competitions, and colorful parades that showcase the rich Samoan heritage.
  • Teuila Festival (Tutuila Island, September) – The Teuila Festival is a week-long extravaganza held in September that celebrates Samoan culture through traditional dance, music, arts and crafts, sports competitions, and a highly anticipated fire knife dancing competition.
  • Aloha Festival (Various Islands, July) – The Aloha Festival, typically held in July, is a joyful celebration of Polynesian culture featuring hula dancing, traditional feasts, canoe races, and vibrant floral parades that capture the essence of island life.
  • Christmas in the Park (Pago Pago, December) – American Samoa comes alive during the holiday season with Christmas in the Park festivities that include carol singing, cultural performances, food stalls offering traditional Samoan cuisine, and a cheerful atmosphere that warms the heart.
  • White Sunday (Various Churches, October) – White Sunday is a special day in American Samoa when children dress in their finest white attire and participate in church services, recitals, and performances to honor and celebrate the importance of youth within the community.
