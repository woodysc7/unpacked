“India is not a regional footnote”: UK’s Indian diaspora rejects ‘South Asian’ label
In a sharp and growing rebuke to Western academic and media narratives, the Indian diaspora in the United Kingdom has forcefully rejected the continued use of the term “South Asian” to describe people of Indian origin, calling it a deliberate erasure of India’s distinct civilisational identity, heritage, and global contributions. Spearheading the movement is Insight UK, a prominent socio-cultural group representing British Hindus and Indian-origin communities, which issued a scathing statement last week on its official social media platform. The group declared that Indians are not a generic subset of South Asia and that the label serves to flatten, distort, and misrepresent a nation of 1.4 billion people with a 5,000-year-old civilisation. “The Indian diaspora rejects the use of the term ‘South Asian,’ which is frequently employed by Western academics and media. They argue that this label, intended as a convenient regional grouping, tends to obscure India’s unique cultural identity rather than celebrate it,” Insight UK wrote. The frustration among Indian-origin Britons reflects an emerging global trend of diaspora assertiveness and cultural reclamation, particularly as Western narratives continue to homogenize vastly different South Asian societies. According to the diaspora, the “South Asian” tag lacks cultural, religious, and historical nuance. The term indiscriminately lumps India — with its profound Dharmic traditions of Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism — with countries like Pakistan and Bangladesh, which were created as religious nation-states during the 1947 Partition. Insight UK noted that this not only undermines the Hindu and Dharmic heritage of India but also equates it with nations that have actively persecuted their minority Hindu populations. The generalisation, they argue, is not only misleading but offensive, especially in academic, political, and media discourses where identity and representation matter deeply. To draw a comparative analogy, the group stated, “It would be akin to conflating Germany and Albania simply because both are in Europe, ignoring vast differences in language, culture, and history.” Social media erupted in support of Insight UK’s stand, with thousands of Indian-origin users echoing the sentiment that the “South Asian” identity is used selectively by the West to either share Indian successes or isolate its shortcomings. “If Indians do good: South Asians. If others do good: Their country. If India does bad: India. If others do bad: South Asians. It’s a whole narrative,” wrote one user on X. Another user remarked, “India is 1.4 billion people — probably worthy of its own term.” There was also frustration at the colonial hangover in Western academia, which still chooses to see India through the lens of British-era mappings rather than acknowledging it as a civilisational state with its own global footprint. The Indian community, with over 2 million members, is the largest ethnic minority in the UK, and one of the most prosperous and educated. Yet, Indian-origin voices are frequently clubbed into South Asian panels, identity blocks, or cultural representations, where nuanced Indian positions are overshadowed by Pakistan-centric or pan-Islamic narratives. Insight UK strongly objected to this intellectual laziness, stating that “being Indian is a matter of pride and a genuine reflection of their heritage — not merely a regional designation.” They further warned that this reductionist trend may have long-term implications for policy-making, representation, and diaspora cohesion. The growing discomfort with the term “South Asian” among Indian-origin Britons is not a fringe sentiment. It is part of a larger movement to reclaim identity, assert historical truth, and resist lazy academic groupings that ignore civilisational boundaries and lived cultural realities. As British institutions — from media houses to universities — continue to use the umbrella term for convenience, diaspora leaders are demanding a rethink, urging the West to recognise Indians as Indians, not as an indistinguishable part of a region. “Indians are Indians. Not South Asians. Not generic brown people. Not a monolith. And definitely not a label to be used when convenient,” said a British-Indian scholar responding to the debate.
A Brief History of Albania

Illyrian and Roman Influence (Before 395 CE)

Albania's ancient history is intertwined with Illyrian tribes who inhabited the region, known for their warrior culture and skilled craftsmanship. The Illyrians interacted with Greek colonies and later came under Roman rule in 168 BCE, contributing to the area's cultural and linguistic evolution. The Romans established cities and infrastructure, leaving a lasting impact on Albania's landscape and society.

Byzantine Era and Slavic Invasions (395–1347)

Following the division of the Roman Empire, Albania fell under Byzantine control, fostering a strong Christian presence. However, waves of Slavic invasions in the 6th and 7th centuries disrupted the region's stability. The Byzantine Empire's influence remained significant, shaping Albania's religious and political landscape during this period.

Ottoman Rule and Cultural Synthesis (1385–1912)

The Ottoman Empire conquered Albania in the late 14th century, marking the beginning of over four centuries of Ottoman rule. This period saw a blending of Albanian and Ottoman cultures, with Albanians adopting Islam and contributing to the empire's military and administrative systems. Despite periods of unrest, Albanian identity and traditions continued to evolve under Ottoman influence.

Independence and the Interwar Period (1912–1939)

Albania declared its independence in 1912, emerging from the collapse of the Ottoman Empire. The country faced internal strife and external pressures, including occupation during World War I. The interwar period was marked by political instability, territorial disputes, and attempts at modernization amidst a challenging geopolitical environment.

Communist Era and Isolation (1944–1991)

After World War II, Albania came under communist rule led by Enver Hoxha, who established a totalitarian regime. Hoxha's policies isolated Albania from the rest of the world, implementing strict controls over all aspects of society. The country experienced economic hardship, political repression, and ideological indoctrination during this period.

Transition to Democracy and European Integration (1991–Present)

The fall of communism in 1991 marked a new chapter for Albania as it transitioned to a democratic system. The country embraced market reforms and pursued closer ties with the West, seeking integration into European institutions. Challenges such as corruption, organized crime, and political polarization have persisted, but Albania continues to make strides towards greater stability, democracy, and prosperity.

Languages of Albania

Languages Spoken in Albania

Albania is a multilingual country with its official language being Albanian. In addition to Albanian, several minority languages are spoken across the country, reflecting its diverse cultural heritage. Here are some of the key languages you may encounter in Albania:

Albanian (Shqip)

Shqip – Albanian is the official language of Albania and is spoken by the majority of the population. It is also one of the official languages in Kosovo and North Macedonia.

Minority Languages

Albania is also home to various minority languages spoken by different ethnic groups. Some of these languages include:

  • Greek – Ellinika
  • Italian – Italiano
  • Macedonian – Makedonski
  • Serbian – Srpski
  • Aromanian – Aromână
  • Romani – Romani

Navigating Language Diversity

While Albanian is the dominant language, in tourist areas and larger cities, you are likely to find people who speak English, especially in hotels, restaurants, and shops. However, learning a few basic Albanian phrases can go a long way in showing respect for the local culture and enhancing your travel experience.

Useful Albanian Phrases

  • Mirëdita – Good day
  • Faleminderit – Thank you
  • Po – Yes
  • Jo – No
  • Ju lutem
Albania Cuisine

Cuisine

Albanian cuisine may not be as widely known as its Mediterranean neighbors, but it offers a delightful mix of flavors influenced by its history and geography. Expect hearty dishes with a focus on fresh, local ingredients like vegetables, meats, and dairy products. Albanian food tends to be simple yet flavorful, showcasing a blend of Mediterranean and Balkan culinary traditions.

  • Tave Kosi – A traditional Albanian dish consisting of baked lamb or veal with rice and yogurt.
  • Byrek – Flaky pastry filled with various ingredients like spinach, cheese, or meat.
  • Fërgesë – A tasty stew made with peppers, tomatoes, and cottage cheese, often served with bread.
  • Qofte – Albanian meatballs seasoned with herbs and spices, served with a side of salad or bread.
  • Baklava – Sweet pastry layers filled with nuts and honey syrup, a popular dessert in Albania.
  • Shishqebap – Grilled skewers of meat (usually lamb or chicken) served with flatbread and salad.
  • Flia – Layered pancake dish often served during celebrations or special occasions.
  • Janë me mish – Stuffed peppers with minced meat and rice, a flavorful Albanian comfort food.
  • Tavë Elbasani – Casserole dish with lamb, eggs, and yogurt, named after the city of Elbasan.

Mealtimes

Albanians typically have a light breakfast, with a focus on coffee and pastries. Lunch is the main meal of the day and is enjoyed leisurely, often including multiple courses. Dinner is lighter and usually eaten later in the evening, reflecting the Mediterranean lifestyle.

Albania Festivals

What are Albania’s most famous festivals?

Albania’s festivals are a vibrant reflection of its rich cultural heritage, showcasing traditional music, dance, food, and customs. Immerse yourself in the lively atmosphere of these iconic celebrations:

  • Tirana International Film Festival (Tirana, November) – A prestigious event that attracts filmmakers and cinema enthusiasts from around the world. Enjoy screenings of diverse films, workshops, and industry networking opportunities.
  • Kala Festival (Dhërmi, June) – An electronic music festival held on the stunning Albanian Riviera. Dance to international DJs, enjoy beach parties, and soak in the breathtaking coastal views.
  • Gjirokastër National Folklore Festival (Gjirokastër, May) – Experience the vibrant traditional music, dance, and crafts of Albania at this annual festival in the UNESCO-listed city of Gjirokastër. Don’t miss the colorful parades and performances.
  • Beer Festival (Korça, August) – Celebrate Albania’s growing craft beer scene at this fun festival featuring local brews, live music, and delicious street food. Join the jovial crowds for a taste of Albanian beer culture.
  • Rrugëtimi Folk Festival (Shkodër, September) – A celebration of Albanian folk music and dance, Rrugëtimi brings together artists and performers from across the country for a joyous showcase of traditional arts.

Festival Tips

When attending festivals in Albania, consider booking accommodations in advance, especially during peak festival seasons. Embrace the local customs and immerse yourself in the festivities. Respect the traditions and cultural practices of the communities hosting the events. Pack comfortable clothing, sunscreen, and a camera to capture memorable moments. Above all, be open to new experiences and connect with the warmth and hospitality of Albanian culture.
