A Brief History of Panama

Pre-Colombian Panama (Before 1501)

Long before the arrival of European explorers, the narrow land bridge that is now Panama was inhabited by various indigenous groups, including the Cueva, Cocle, and Chibcha peoples. These early civilizations thrived through farming, fishing, and trading, establishing complex societies and cultural traditions that would shape Panama's identity for centuries to come.

Spanish Conquest and Colonial Era (1501–1821)

In 1501, during his fourth and final voyage, Christopher Columbus reached the shores of Panama. The Spanish conquistadors, led by Vasco Núñez de Balboa, soon followed, seeking gold and new territories. Balboa's discovery of the Pacific Ocean in 1513 marked a turning point, as Panama became a vital hub for transporting riches from South America to Spain. The construction of the Panama Canal during the colonial era further solidified Panama's strategic importance.

Independence and Union with Gran Colombia (1821–1903)

Panama, along with other Spanish colonies in Latin America, declared independence in 1821. Initially part of Gran Colombia, a federation led by Simón Bolívar, Panama later joined the Republic of New Granada in 1831. Throughout the 19th century, Panama's desire for autonomy and economic development grew, eventually leading to the separation from Colombia in 1903 with the support of the United States.

Building the Panama Canal (1904–1914)

The United States took over the construction of the Panama Canal in 1904, after an unsuccessful attempt by the French in the late 19th century. The canal, completed in 1914, revolutionized global trade by connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. Its construction brought significant economic benefits to Panama but also led to social and political tensions, as the canal zone was under U.S. control until 1999.

Military Coups and Dictatorship (1968–1989)

Panama experienced a period of political instability in the late 20th century, characterized by military coups and authoritarian rule. General Omar Torrijos took power in a 1968 coup and initiated social reforms but ruled with an iron fist. After Torrijos's death in a plane crash in 1981, General Manuel Noriega rose to power, leading a repressive regime marked by corruption and human rights abuses.

Return to Democracy and Panama Canal Handover (1989–Present)

In 1989, the United States invaded Panama to remove Noriega from power, marking the end of military rule. Subsequently, Panama transitioned back to democracy, holding free elections and establishing a more stable political system. The handover of the Panama Canal to Panama in 1999 symbolized the country's sovereignty and marked a new chapter in its history, focusing on economic diversification and continued development.

Languages of Panama

Languages Spoken in Panama

Panama is a country with a rich linguistic landscape reflecting its diverse cultural influences. The official language is Spanish, spoken by the majority of the population. However, due to its history and geographic location, you'll also encounter other languages in Panama.

Indigenous Languages

Panama is home to several indigenous groups, each with its own language. Some of the indigenous languages spoken in Panama include Ngäbere, Guna, Emberá, and Buglé. These languages are integral to the cultural heritage of the respective communities.

English in Panama

English is widely understood in Panama, particularly in urban areas and tourist destinations. It is commonly spoken in the business sector and by younger generations. Around 14% of the Panamanian population speaks English, with higher proficiency in urban centers and among those in the tourism industry.

Panama Cuisine

Cuisine

Panamanian cuisine offers a delightful blend of flavors influenced by its diverse cultural heritage, including indigenous, Spanish, African, and Caribbean traditions. While not as internationally renowned as some of its Latin American neighbors, Panama's food scene is a hidden gem waiting to be explored. The cuisine here tends to be hearty and comforting, with a focus on fresh ingredients like seafood, tropical fruits, and root vegetables. You'll find a mix of flavors that range from savory to sweet, with a touch of spice in some dishes.

  • Sancocho – A traditional stew made with chicken, vegetables, and herbs, often considered Panama's national dish.
  • Ceviche – Fresh seafood marinated in citrus juices, typically served with onions, peppers, and cilantro.
  • Ropa Vieja – Shredded beef stewed in a savory tomato-based sauce, served with rice and beans.
  • Patacones – Fried plantains, smashed and then fried again, often served as a side dish or snack.
  • Hojaldres – Crispy fried dough similar to a savory puff pastry, enjoyed with breakfast or as a snack.
  • Arroz con Pollo – A flavorful rice and chicken dish cooked with vegetables and spices.
  • Ceviche de Mango – A refreshing twist on ceviche, using mango instead of seafood for a sweet and tangy flavor.
  • Tamal de Maíz – Corn tamales filled with meat, vegetables, and olives, wrapped in corn husks and steamed.
  • Raspados – Shaved ice desserts topped with flavored syrups, condensed milk, and fruit.

Panama Festivals

What are Panama’s most famous festivals?

Panama’s rich cultural heritage is showcased through its vibrant and diverse festivals that reflect the country's history, traditions, and lively spirit. Here are some of the most iconic celebrations you won’t want to miss:

  • Carnival de Panamá (Panama City, February) – A vibrant and lively celebration leading up to Lent, featuring colorful parades, traditional music, dancing, elaborate costumes, and lively street parties that last for days.
  • Festival del Diablos y Congos (Portobelo, October) – A unique festival blending African, Spanish, and Indigenous traditions, with colorful devil masks, drumming, dancing, and processions honoring the town's patron saint, the Black Christ of Portobelo.
  • Independence Day Celebrations (Nationwide, November) – Commemorating Panama's independence from Spain in 1821, this national holiday features patriotic parades, traditional music and dances, fireworks, and cultural events throughout the country.
  • Feria de Azuero (La Villa de Los Santos, April) – A traditional folk festival celebrating Panama’s cultural heritage with folkloric dances, music, artisanal crafts, bullfighting, rodeo shows, and traditional culinary delights.
  • Festival del Manito Ocueño (Ocú, September) – A lively festival honoring the region's patron saint, featuring traditional dances, music, processions, horseback riding competitions, and the famous "mojadera" water fights.

Festival Tips

When attending festivals in Panama, embrace the vibrant atmosphere and immerse yourself in the local traditions. Make sure to sample traditional Panamanian cuisine and beverages. Respect cultural norms and dress modestly when required. Plan your accommodations in advance as popular festivals attract visitors from near and far. Stay hydrated, wear sunscreen, and be prepared for warm weather conditions. Most importantly, show respect for the local customs and enjoy the unique cultural experiences Panama has to offer.
