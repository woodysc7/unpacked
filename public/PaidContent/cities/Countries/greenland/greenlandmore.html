A Brief History of Greenland

Early Inhabitants and Norse Settlements (Pre-986)

Greenland’s history dates back to the arrival of Paleo-Eskimo peoples around 2500 BCE. These early inhabitants, including the Dorset culture, thrived in the harsh Arctic environment for millennia. In the late 10th century, Norse explorers, led by Erik the Red, established settlements on the island's southwestern coast. These Norse settlements, such as Brattahlid and Gardar, marked the first known European presence in Greenland.

The Norse Colonization and Contact with Europe (986–15th Century)

The Norse colonies in Greenland, known collectively as the Eastern Settlement and the Western Settlement, flourished for several centuries. They traded ivory, furs, and walrus tusks with Europe, particularly Norway and Iceland. However, by the 15th century, the Norse settlements faced challenges including climate change, declining trade, and possible conflict with the Inuit population, leading to their eventual abandonment.

Inuit Expansion and European Exploration (15th–18th Century)

Following the departure of the Norse, the Inuit expanded into the vacated areas and established their own thriving communities. Meanwhile, European explorers like John Davis, William Baffin, and Henry Hudson began mapping Greenland’s coastline and seeking the elusive Northwest Passage. Danish explorers, including Vitus Bering, also made significant contributions to the understanding of Greenland's geography during this period.

Danish Colonization and Modern History (18th Century–Present)

In the early 18th century, Denmark-Norway established control over Greenland, with Danish trading posts and missions appearing along the coast. The colonization intensified in the 19th and 20th centuries, with the establishment of towns like Godthaab (now Nuuk) and the introduction of modern infrastructure. Greenland remained a Danish colony until gaining home rule in 1979 and self-government in 2009, while still maintaining ties to the Danish crown. Today, Greenland stands at a crossroads of tradition and modernity, with a unique blend of indigenous Inuit culture and Danish influences shaping its identity.

Languages of Greenland

Languages of Greenland

Greenland is a multilingual country with Greenlandic (Kalaallisut) being the official language. Danish is also widely spoken and serves as the second official language due to Greenland's historical ties to Denmark. In addition to these, English is increasingly used, especially in urban areas and among younger generations.

Local Dialects and Regional Languages

In different regions of Greenland, various dialects of the Greenlandic language are spoken, reflecting the diverse cultural heritage across the island. These dialects may vary in pronunciation and vocabulary, but speakers can generally understand each other. Additionally, some communities may use Inuktun or Inuktitut alongside Greenlandic for communication.

Respecting the Language and Culture

Learning a few basic phrases in Greenlandic, Danish, or Inuktun can greatly enhance your experience and show respect for the local culture. Greenlandic is a challenging language to learn, but attempting to use it, even in simple greetings, is appreciated by locals and can create positive interactions.

Greenland Cuisine

Cuisine

Greenlandic cuisine reflects the harsh Arctic environment and the traditional Inuit way of life. While the food may seem simple to some, it holds a deep cultural significance and provides a unique culinary experience for visitors. The cuisine primarily consists of seafood, game meats, and foraged plants, showcasing the resourcefulness of the Inuit people in utilizing what the land and sea provide. Expect dishes that are hearty, rich in protein and nutrients, and often enjoyed in the company of locals who are eager to share their traditions.

  • Kiviak – A delicacy made from whole auks (birds) fermented in a seal skin, a traditional winter dish.
  • Suaasat – A traditional Greenlandic soup made with seal, whale, or reindeer meat, often mixed with onions and potatoes.
  • Amassat – Dried fish, typically halibut, that is rehydrated and served with a side of seal blubber.
  • Seal Jerky – Thin strips of dried seal meat, a popular snack for its high protein content.
  • Arctic Char – Freshwater fish similar to salmon, commonly grilled or smoked.
  • Reindeer Stew – Slow-cooked reindeer meat with root vegetables, a comforting and hearty dish.
  • Blueberries – Foraged wild blueberries that are a common ingredient in desserts and jams.
  • Kalaallit Kaagiat – Traditional Greenlandic pancakes made with flour, sugar, and water, often served with butter or jam.
  • Greenlandic Coffee – Coffee spiked with whiskey and Grand Marnier, a popular after-dinner drink to warm up in the cold climate.

Greenland Festivals

What are Greenland’s most famous festivals?

Greenland’s festivals are a unique blend of traditional Inuit culture and modern celebrations, offering visitors a glimpse into the rich heritage of this Arctic land. Here are some of the most notable festivals to experience:

  • Kaffemik (Throughout Greenland, Year-round) – A beloved Inuit tradition where locals invite friends, family, and visitors into their homes for coffee, cake, and conversation. It's a warm and welcoming way to connect with the community.
  • Qaqqat (Various Locations, Winter) – This winter festival celebrates the return of the sun after months of darkness. Expect dog sledding races, ice fishing competitions, traditional drum dancing, and feasts of local delicacies.
  • Ullortuneq (Nuuk, February) – Greenland's National Day is a vibrant celebration of culture and identity. Enjoy traditional performances, craft markets, and delicious Greenlandic cuisine, all against the backdrop of stunning Arctic landscapes.
  • Arctic Sounds Festival (Ilulissat, June) – A music festival like no other, featuring local and international artists performing under the midnight sun. Enjoy the fusion of contemporary music with the raw beauty of Greenland’s natural surroundings.
  • Great Fish Day (Qeqertarsuaq, July) – A seafood lover’s paradise, this festival showcases the best of Greenlandic fishing culture. Enjoy freshly caught fish, traditional dishes, and lively dancing and music by the sea.

Festival Tips

When attending festivals in Greenland, be prepared for changing weather conditions and pack layers to stay warm. Respect local customs and traditions, and participate in activities with an open heart and mind. Bring a camera to capture the unique moments and stunning landscapes, and don’t forget to try the local cuisine for a true taste of Greenlandic culture.
