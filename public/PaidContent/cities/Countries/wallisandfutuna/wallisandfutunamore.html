A Brief History of Wallis and Futuna

Early Settlement and Polynesian Influence (Before 16th Century)

Wallis and Futuna, located in the South Pacific, were likely settled by Polynesian peoples around 1000 BCE. These early settlers brought their unique culture, language, and traditions to the islands, establishing a way of life closely tied to the surrounding ocean.

European Contact and Missionary Activity (16th–19th Century)

In the 16th century, European explorers, primarily from France and Britain, began to visit the islands. By the 19th century, French missionaries had a significant influence on the local population, leading to the conversion of many islanders to Christianity. This period marked the beginning of European involvement in the region.

Colonial Rule and Integration into French Overseas Territories (19th–20th Century)

During the 19th century, the islands of Wallis and Futuna came under the control of France. The French established colonial administration, bringing changes to local governance and society. In 1961, the islands officially became a French overseas territory, solidifying their ties to France and integrating them into the French political and economic system.

World War II and Post-War Developments (20th Century)

During World War II, Wallis and Futuna were briefly occupied by Free French forces to prevent them from falling into Japanese hands. After the war, the islands experienced some modernization and infrastructure development, improving living conditions for the local population. However, the traditional way of life and cultural practices remained strong despite these changes.

Contemporary Autonomy and Cultural Preservation (21st Century)

In recent years, there has been a growing movement towards autonomy and self-governance in Wallis and Futuna. The islands have been granted a certain degree of self-rule within the French Republic, allowing them to preserve their unique cultural heritage while also benefiting from French support and resources. Today, Wallis and Futuna continue to balance traditional customs with modern influences, creating a distinctive identity in the Pacific region.

Languages of Wallis and Futuna

Languages Spoken in Wallis and Futuna

Wallis and Futuna, a French overseas territory in the Pacific, has two official languages: French and Wallisian (also known as Wallisian-Futunan or East Uvean). These languages reflect the unique cultural blend of Polynesian and French influences in the territory.

English in Wallis and Futuna

English proficiency in Wallis and Futuna is limited, with only a small percentage of the population speaking English. While French is more commonly used for communication, especially in official settings, some basic English may be understood in tourist areas due to interactions with English-speaking visitors.

Basic Wallisian Phrases

  • Mālō – Hello
  • Fānongo faka’ofa – Goodbye
  • Fakafeta’i – Thank you
  • ‘Io – Yes
  • Talitali kiai – Excuse me / Sorry
  • ‘A e aha ia? – What is this?
  • Fakamolemole – Please
  • Fēfē hake? – How much?
  • Fakamalie atu – You’re welcome
  • ‘Oku ou fie ma’u atu ha me’a – I would like to buy something
  • ‘Oku ou fiema’u ha ongoongo – I need information
Wallis and Futuna Cuisine

Cuisine

Wallis and Futuna, a remote French territory in the South Pacific, offers a unique blend of Polynesian and French influences in its cuisine. While the food may not be as widely known as other Pacific Island cuisines, it still holds its own charm with a focus on fresh, simple ingredients and traditional cooking methods. Expect a delightful mix of seafood, tropical fruits, root vegetables, and French pastries during your culinary exploration of these islands.

  • Poisson Cru – A traditional Polynesian dish of raw fish marinated in lime juice and coconut milk, similar to ceviche.
  • Lapulu – Taro leaves cooked in coconut milk, often served alongside roasted pork or fish.
  • Poisson Ufi – Fish wrapped in banana leaves and baked in an earth oven, infusing flavors of the tropics.
  • Gateau Patate – Sweet potato cake, a popular dessert in Wallis and Futuna, showcasing local produce.
  • Firi Firi – Deep-fried doughnut-like pastries, a beloved snack on the islands.
  • Langouste Grillée – Grilled lobster, a luxurious treat for seafood lovers in this marine-rich region.
  • Po'e – A sweet pudding made from bananas, taro, or breadfruit, a satisfying end to a meal.
  • Coconut Crab – A local delicacy, the meat of this giant crab is rich and flavorful.
  • Mitaki – Roasted breadfruit, a staple carbohydrate in the Polynesian diet.

Mealtimes

In Wallis and Futuna, meals are a communal affair, often enjoyed with family and friends. Lunch is the main meal of the day, usually served around midday and featuring a variety of dishes. Dinner is lighter and typically eaten later in the evening, allowing time for relaxation and socializing after a day's work or exploration of the islands.

Wallis and Futuna Festivals

What are Wallis and Futuna’s most famous festivals?

The vibrant culture of Wallis and Futuna comes alive during its unique and colorful festivals, offering visitors a glimpse into the rich traditions of this Polynesian paradise. While the islands may be small, their celebrations are big on heart and spirit:

  • Fete du Territoire (Wallis Island, May) – Celebrated in May, this festival showcases traditional dances, music, and feasting, offering a wonderful opportunity to immerse yourself in the local culture.
  • Festival of Uvea (Uvea Island, November) – Held in November, this festival honors the heritage and customs of Uvea through dance performances, canoe races, and delicious feasts of traditional dishes.
  • Feast of St. Pierre Chanel (Futuna Island, April) – In April, Futuna Island comes alive with festivities to honor St. Pierre Chanel, the patron saint of the island. Expect processions, church services, and lively village gatherings.
  • Wallis and Futuna Day (Wallis and Futuna, July) – Celebrated on July 29th, this national holiday commemorates the islands' history and culture with parades, traditional games, arts, and crafts exhibitions, and a festive atmosphere throughout the islands.
  • Feast of the Assumption (Futuna Island, August) – In August, the Feast of the Assumption is a significant religious event on Futuna Island, marked by church services, processions, and traditional ceremonies that reflect the island's deep-rooted faith and customs.

Festival Tips

When attending festivals in Wallis and Futuna, embrace the warm hospitality of the locals and participate in the cultural activities with respect and enthusiasm. Pack light, breathable clothing suitable for the tropical climate, and don’t forget sunscreen and insect repellent. Be prepared for traditional Polynesian food offerings and be open to trying new flavors. Lastly, take the time to learn about the customs and traditions of the islands to fully appreciate the significance of each festival experience.
